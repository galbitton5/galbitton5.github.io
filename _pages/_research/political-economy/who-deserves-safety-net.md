  <p class="project-title">“Who Deserves a Safety Net? Labour-Market Dualization and Moral Heuristics.” With Gianluca Busilacchi. </p>
  <p class="project-links">
    <a class="toggle-abstract">▶ Abstract</a>
  </p>
  <div class="abstract">
    <p>Does labor-market vulnerability unite outsiders around a shared sense of welfare solidarity? Prominent insider--outsider accounts treat unemployed and precariously employed people as a common constituency, but this expectation overlooks a basic distinction: exclusion from employment and insecure attachment to employment may generate different moral orientations. Combining the European Social Survey (N=33,449) with OECD employment-protection indicators, we examine how labor-market positions are associated with punitive judgments of welfare claimants and whether the regulatory gap between permanent and temporary employment reshapes these judgments. Among the conventional outsider positions, currently unemployed respondents are substantially less punitive than otherwise comparable permanent-contract workers, consistent with solidarity rooted in direct exposure to unemployment. Fixed-term and no-contract workers, however, do not exhibit the same greater sympathy. Most notably, as the protection gap increases, fixed-term workers become more punitive relative to permanent-contract workers, and this pattern is not driven by any single country. The greater sympathy shown by currently unemployed respondents is similar in countries with narrower and wider protection gaps. Non-employed people with past unemployment become less punitive as the gap widens, although this pattern is not as consistent across countries. These findings challenge the assumption that shared vulnerability generates a common outsider outlook: direct unemployment is associated with greater sympathy, whereas insecure employment does not produce the same solidarity and may become more punitive where regulatory dualization is sharper.</p>
