Political Violence and Threat
----
<div class="project">
  <p class="project-title">“Assembling Security Consensus: Political Violence, Ideology, and Support for State Surveillance.” With Daphna Canetti, Giulia Dal Bello, Julia Elad-Strenger, and Sivan Hirsch-Hoefler. (<em>Revise & Resubmit, Journal of Peace Research</em>) </p>
  <p class="project-links">
    <a class="toggle-abstract">▶ Abstract</a>
  </p>
  <div class="abstract">
    <p>How does personal exposure to political violence shape citizens' willingness to expand state security power during conflict? Research shows that wartime violence affects democratic commitments unevenly, yet we know less about how it assembles public support for intrusive security policies. We argue that exposure to violence changes which ideological constituencies become available for security consensus. Where support for state security capacity is already high, exposure should leave attitudes largely unchanged. Among centrists, exposure may give security concerns greater weight alongside commitments drawn from across ideological camps. Where surveillance conflicts with civil-liberty commitments, the association should depend on the threat environment and the plausible relevance of domestic monitoring to that threat. We examine these expectations using two original repeated cross-sectional surveys of Jewish Israelis fielded during distinct phases of the multi-front conflict that began on October 7, 2023 (N = 2,199). The findings show that exposure among centrists is consistently associated with greater support, narrowing their gap with the political right and broadening the constituencies supporting surveillance. Among right-wing respondents, already high levels of surveillance support remain largely unchanged with exposure. Among left-wing respondents, the exposure--surveillance association differs across surveys relative to the right-wing baseline: exposed leftists converge toward the right in 2024, when non-state violence dominated the threat environment, but not in 2025, after the conflict broadened to include direct confrontation with a foreign state. Despite claims that political violence broadly pushes citizens toward security, we show that wartime security consensus is not a uniform public reaction. It is politically assembled through anchored support on the right, mobilization of the center, and possible limits to convergence on the left.</p>
